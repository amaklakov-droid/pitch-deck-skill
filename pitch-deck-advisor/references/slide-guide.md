# Per-Slide Guide

Detailed guidance for each of the 12 canonical slides. For each slide: what it
must accomplish, how investors actually read it, and the most common failure
modes with fixes.

## 1. Title

**Job:** identify the company and state what it does in one declarative
sentence — before the investor decides whether to keep reading.

- The one-liner is the hardest sentence in the deck. Formula options:
  - Analogy: "X for Y" — only if X is universally known and the analogy is
    honest (Coinbase's analogy saved entire slides of explanation).
  - Plain assertion: "We make [product] that lets [customer] do [outcome]."
- Test: a 5-year-old (or an investor's analyst skimming 40 decks that day)
  understands it.

**Failures:** vision statements instead of descriptions ("Reimagining the
future of work"); jargon ("AI-powered synergistic B2B enablement layer").

## 2. Problem

**Job:** prove a specific, expensive pain exists and is being solved badly.

- Formulate in the customer's world with operational/financial consequences:
  who hurts, how often, what it costs them, how they cope today.
- The "how they cope today" part sets up both Solution and Competition.
- Numbers beat adjectives: "nurses spend 90 min/shift on paperwork" beats
  "healthcare documentation is inefficient."

**Failures:** abstract problems ("the market is fragmented"); problems the
founder finds interesting but no one pays to solve; three unrelated problems
(pick the one your beachhead customer bleeds from).

## 3. Solution

**Job:** make the value proposition obvious in 10 seconds.

- Show, don't tell: product screenshots, demo frames, a photo of working
  hardware. For deep tech, video of working (and being repaired) hardware
  beats CAD renders — it shows engineering endurance.
- Map directly onto the Problem slide: each pain named there should visibly
  disappear here.
- State where the product physically lives (app, API, device, service).

**Failures:** feature lists instead of outcomes; architecture diagrams (those
go to slide 6 or the appendix); solving a different problem than slide 2
stated.

## 4. Why Now

**Job:** answer "why couldn't this exist 5 years ago, and why will waiting 5
years be too late?"

- Name the specific shift: a technology crossing a cost/performance threshold,
  a regulation changing, a behavior change, an infrastructure buildout.
- Critical at pre-seed (often the most-read slide); barely read at Series A —
  by then traction answers the question.

**Failures:** generic tailwinds ("AI is booming"); no answer to "why didn't
the last 10 startups that tried this succeed?"

## 5. Market

**Job:** show the prize is large AND that you know precisely who buys first.

- TAM top-down is acceptable as context; **SAM must be bottom-up**: (number of
  target customers) × (realistic annual contract value). Show the arithmetic.
- Define the customer profile concretely: segment, size, geography, budget
  owner.
- A credible $300M bottom-up SAM beats a hand-waved $50B TAM.

**Failures:** "if we capture 1% of the $500B market"; conflating users with
buyers; market defined so broadly the GTM slide becomes impossible.

## 6. Product / Technology

**Job:** explain how it works well enough to be credible, without drowning the
reader.

- No untranslated jargon — every technical term must be converted into a
  customer value or a moat claim.
- IP summary belongs here or on a dedicated moat slide (mandatory for deep
  tech — see deep-tech.md): patents filed/granted, unique know-how, datasets.
- Roadmap: 3–5 milestones max, tied to the ask.
- Depth (architecture diagrams, benchmark tables, specs) goes to the appendix.

**Failures:** the "visual essay" — a deck-inside-a-deck of technical detail;
tech described with no link to why customers pay.

## 7. Traction

**Job:** prove momentum. This slide and Team get the most investor attention
(~45–50 seconds of a 2.5-minute read).

- One clean chart, up and to the right, honest axes. Revenue > active usage >
  pilots > LOIs > waitlist, in order of strength.
- Pre-seed/seed without revenue: use signal traction — named pilots with
  expansion options, conversion of pilots to paid, retention of early users.
- Include the metric's definition if there's any ambiguity (what counts as
  "active"?). An investor who gets stuck on this slide (spending 2× the time)
  isn't impressed — they're confused, and confusion kills deals.

**Failures:** ten metrics on one chart; cumulative charts hiding flat growth;
vanity metrics (signups, page views, app downloads without retention).

## 8. Business Model

**Job:** show exactly one credible way money flows to you.

- One model. Alternatives are "future potential," mentioned in one line at
  most.
- Pricing with a real number, and the unit economics you have: gross margin,
  CAC/LTV if measured, payback period. Early stage: it's fine to show the
  target economics with assumptions labeled as assumptions.

**Failures:** three models "to be tested"; pricing "TBD"; LTV computed on 3
months of data presented as fact.

## 9. Competition

**Job:** prove you understand the landscape and have a durable edge.

- Include the status quo as a competitor: Excel, manual labor, "do nothing."
- Preferred formats: 2×2 with axes that matter to customers (not "us = top
  right of everything"), or a capability table where your advantage is
  structural, not feature-count.
- The advantage must be durable: data moat, IP, network effects, switching
  costs, regulatory position — not "better UX" alone.
- If an obvious strategic acquirer exists, mention them here as a competitor —
  never as an exit slide.

**Failures:** "we have no competitors"; dishonest feature tables; listing only
startups while ignoring the incumbent status quo.

## 10. Go-to-Market

**Job:** show the concrete path to the first customers and the repeatable
motion after them. One of the most commonly missing slides.

- Name: first segment (beachhead), channel, tactic, sales cycle length,
  who sells, and the timeline.
- Early proof beats theory: "3 of our 5 pilots came from regulatory
  consultants; we're formalizing that referral channel" beats a funnel
  diagram.

**Failures:** "marketing + sales + partnerships" (a list of nouns is not a
strategy); GTM that doesn't match the market slide's customer profile.

## 11. Team

**Job:** answer "why will *these people* win *this market*?" — founder-market
fit. On early-stage decks investors spend the most time here; they invest in
people.

- For each founder: the 1–2 facts that make them unfairly suited to this
  problem (domain years, prior exits, published research, built X at Y).
- Show the team covers the critical risks: technical + commercial for deep
  tech.
- Prior collaboration ("worked together 4 years at X") de-risks the team
  itself.
- Advisors: only if genuinely engaged and relevant; logos-for-decoration are
  transparent.

**Failures:** photo gallery with job titles and universities; omitting the
obvious question (e.g., no commercial founder in a B2B company) instead of
addressing it.

## 12. Financials + Ask

**Job:** state how much you're raising and what it makes true.

- **One number.** No ranges, no "flexible," no valuation.
- Tie to milestones, not runway: "This $2.5M gets us to pilot validation with
  partner X, proving assumption Y at commercial scale" — not "18 months of
  runway."
- Use of funds in 3–4 buckets (%, not line-item budgets).
- Financial projections: 3 years max, tied to drivers the deck already
  established; label assumptions.

**Failures:** ask ranges; valuation on the slide; hockey sticks with no
driver model; for hardware — an ask too *small* for the stated production
goals (as much a red flag as too large).

## 13. Appendix

**Job:** absorb everything that would overload the core deck, ready for the
second meeting.

- Technical architecture, benchmark data, full financial model summary, pilot
  results, detailed competitive analysis, team bios.
- For Series A, consider a separate data deck instead (metrics detail for due
  diligence).
